The last performance of an old travelling puppet theatre. Two living marionettes lead the story: its proud, accomplished female dancer and a shy male backstage clown who sweeps, carries scenery and has never shared the curtain call. She wants one perfect farewell solo; he keeps finding his way into her dance while trying to do his work.

The opening is a comic contest: a runaway hat, a stool claimed by both, an overambitious bow, interrupted dance steps and a broom that gets in the way. She sings with elegant confidence; his warm sung replies and improvised movements keep upsetting her perfect routine.

A storm catches the canvas roof. Scenery rolls and swings, a ladder slips and a support begins falling towards the clown. The dancer gets him clear, but the step beneath her breaks. He catches the loose end of her long red costume sash and throws the other end within her reach. Both must hold it and coordinate their weight: a hard pull would unbalance the other. Their rescue becomes a dance lesson. She teaches him through her movements; his improvisations find routes across the damaged stage.

In the final chorus, the familiar dance becomes a shared waltz over the remaining boards. Their theatre still closes; a miraculous financial rescue is not invented. At the end, he automatically collects his broom and withdraws into the wings. She takes his hand, brings him beside her, and waits until he understands. They bow together. Both are alive; the relationship is expressed through what they do for each other.

## Musical contract

Original French lyrics, two clearly differentiated adult voices, fully sung dialogue. A lively 3/4 theatrical waltz with rhythmic comic timing grows into an urgent rescue and an expansive, tender duet. A recurring four-bar hook begins with "Un pas pour toi, un pas pour moi" and changes to "Un pas vers toi, un pas vers moi" while retaining its melody. The last two chorus lines remember that this is the theatre's final night.

The instrumental chase and rescue passages leave room for the puppets to act. The first chorus's dance is competitive, the second becomes careful reciprocal balance, and the last is willing partnership. The final exchange makes the clown's first shared bow explicit. ## Staging commitments

A repeated root hop is not a dance. Retain the physical card/rod/string theatre and its visible machinery.

Establish each character's movement vocabulary, then let them learn from one another. Both characters act and both contribute to the rescue. Keep the dancers readable through the storm and scenery changes.
