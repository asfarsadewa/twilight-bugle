The last performance of an old travelling puppet theatre. Two living marionettes lead the story: its proud, accomplished female dancer and a shy male backstage clown who sweeps, carries scenery and has never shared the curtain call. She wants one perfect farewell solo; he keeps finding his way into her dance while trying to do his work.

The opening can play as a satirical contest: a runaway hat, a stool claimed by both, an overambitious bow, interrupted dance steps and a broom that gets in the way. The music remains a lament. Her precision conceals fear of losing her world; his replies carry the ache of someone who has spent his life behind the curtain. Even the amusing physical business sits beneath the knowledge that this is their final night.

A storm catches the canvas roof. Scenery rolls and swings, a ladder slips and a support begins falling towards the clown. The dancer gets him clear, but the step beneath her breaks. He catches the loose end of her long red costume sash and throws the other end within her reach. Both must hold it and coordinate their weight: a hard pull would unbalance the other. Their rescue becomes a dance lesson. She teaches him through her movements; his improvisations find routes across the damaged stage.

In the final chorus, the familiar dance becomes a shared farewell over the remaining boards. Their theatre still closes; a miraculous financial rescue is not invented. At the end, he automatically collects his broom and withdraws into the wings. She takes his hand, brings him beside her, and waits until he understands. They bow together under a grieving score. Both are alive; her invitation is an act of care while their world is ending, with no promise of a happy future. The bow must not become a compensating victory. Let the minor coda register the work that remains—gathering the objects, folding the ribbon, clearing the boards—and the loss of the theatre.
